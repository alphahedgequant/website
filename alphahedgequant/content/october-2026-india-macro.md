# ALPHAHEDGEQUANT | MONTHLY INDIA MACRO
## October 2026 — The Export Breakout Meets the Inflation Test

**Published 13 November 2026 · Research data cut-off: 13 November 2026, 21:00 IST.**

AlphaHedgeQuant Research Desk

**Central thesis.** India has delivered a record merchandise-export month and a more convincing external-services surplus, at the same time that food inflation has pushed headline CPI above 6%, the RBI has tightened policy, and an energy-heavy import bill is testing the trade balance. We are not choosing between the headlines. We are asking whether manufacturing volumes, consumption, capital formation and business cash flows are improving quickly enough to pay for a higher cost of capital.

The research combines three disciplines: scrutiny of primary releases and their dates; a market-risk assessment that challenges comfortable narratives; and a valuation bridge from economic activity to revenues, margins, reinvestment and enterprise value. Conclusions below are conditional hypotheses rather than recommendations or claims of certainty.

### The macro picture at a glance

- **Exports were very strong.** India sold more goods abroad than ever before in a single month, which is encouraging for manufacturing, engineering and export-facing businesses.
- **But the import bill is still heavy.** Oil, gold and other imports kept the total import bill large, so the trade gap did not disappear.
- **Inflation is the immediate pressure point.** Food inflation moved higher, which hurts household purchasing power and keeps pressure on the RBI.
- **Industry is doing better than consumption breadth.** Capital goods, intermediate goods and infrastructure-linked output look stronger than mass non-durable demand.
- **For investors, the key question is quality.** The best businesses are still the ones that can grow volumes, protect margins and generate cash even if interest rates stay high for longer.

**Currency convention.** Trade series are published in USD. Rupee equivalents are illustrative, translated at **₹94.10 per US$1** (indicative 13 November mid-market quote) rather than an official October average; none is an official rupee trade statistic. The report retains USD amounts where essential for cross-border comparability. [S24]

## 01 / The trade surprise: stronger exports, two simultaneous deficits

**What the evidence means.** India had a very good export month, but it still spent a lot on imports. So the story is positive, not perfect.

There are three different trade questions here: Were goods exports better than last year? Did the merchandise deficit improve relative to a year ago? And did India's external requirement improve relative to the previous month? The answers are yes, yes and no. Merchandise exports of $49.47bn were up about 45% YoY from $34.11bn, while imports of $89.35bn were up 16.5% from $76.69bn. On that comparison the merchandise deficit narrowed from $42.58bn to $39.88bn, a 6.3% improvement. But the September deficit was $30.21bn, so October's $39.88bn balance is approximately $9.67bn worse sequentially and is reported as a one-year high. The base and the frequency change the story. [S01, S10]

The services side is a genuine stabiliser: ministry estimates place exports at $40.51bn and imports at $20.72bn, producing a $19.79bn surplus, approximately 13.5% larger than the $17.44bn implied by October 2025's estimates. The combined goods-and-services deficit is therefore estimated at $20.09bn versus $25.15bn a year earlier, a decline near 20%. These numbers are NOT yet October RBI services-trade actuals. The RBI's latest actual series at this cutoff covers September: receipts $39.305bn and payments $20.097bn. Its monthly figure differs from Commerce's earlier September estimate because estimates are revised when RBI data arrive. [S01, S02]

For April–October 2026 (the first seven months of FY2026–27), merchandise exports were around $309.06bn, up roughly 22%; imports were $527.21bn, up around 16.4%. The goods deficit for these seven months was therefore still around $218.15bn. On the overall goods-plus-services measure, the deficit was $93.35bn compared with $83.25bn in the previous year—up around 12%. The circulating statement that “April–October deficit rose only 12%” therefore needs a label: it refers to **overall** trade, not necessarily to goods alone. The vintages also contain revisions; do not add September and October figures from differently revised releases without checking. [S01]

The attractive but unproven inference is that export growth must be volume-led because imports did not rise equally quickly. That does not follow. Imports and exports consist of different products, price bases, contracts, currencies and shipping schedules. Export value equals quantity multiplied by unit value, aggregated across product mix. Faster exports than imports cannot identify which component changed. We need DGCI&S commodity quantities, unit-value indices, constant-price decomposition and product-level comparisons before claiming that exports were principally volume-driven. Petroleum prices and refined-product prices are particularly important. Reported petroleum-import expenditure reached about $22.82bn in October, while gold imports reached roughly $5.51bn. Commodity valuations and festival-related timing can dominate one month. [S01]

Diversification is promising, and faster exports to partner markets deserve attention, but proposed FTA success claims need a matched country-product dataset, a baseline and a control for demand and currency. Singapore belongs to ASEAN; adding Singapore's percentage to an ASEAN aggregate would double-count. Without commodity volumes or a credible comparison group we cannot attribute a change to trade agreements alone.

## 02 / Inflation: a genuine monetary-policy challenge

**What the evidence means.** Prices, especially food prices, are rising fast enough to worry households and the RBI. That means borrowing costs may stay high for longer.

MoSPI's October CPI release, published on 12 November, revised the current information set. Headline CPI inflation rose to 6.09% YoY from September's final 5.54%, a 0.55 **percentage-point** increase. Rural inflation reached 6.63% versus urban 5.41%. Consumer food inflation climbed to 8.94% from 7.74%, with rural food at 9.09%. Both the 2024-base basket and the rural/urban composition matter. Food and beverages represent a substantial expenditure weight, so a food shock can affect disposable real income even as nominal sales continue to expand. These are provisional October statistics, not final data. [S03]

The split across expenditure categories is equally useful. October inflation in food and beverages reached 8.51%, restaurants and accommodation 10.06%, and transport 5.84%, against September readings of 7.36%, 9.36% and 5.01%. This spread raises the possibility of second-round effects, but proves neither a wage-price spiral nor a uniform rise in underlying inflation. Inflation expectations, wages, firm price-setting and disaggregated core CPI still need to be tested. [S03]

Wholesale inflation provides a second lens, but not an October observation yet. The latest published WPI, for September, was 11.39% YoY; fuel and power was 25.85%, manufactured products 9.76%. The October WPI is scheduled for 16 November. It would be an error to combine September WPI and October CPI as though they cover the same observation period. Rising wholesale input costs can precede margin compression if producers cannot pass them through, yet pass-through varies with industry concentration, inventories and contracted pricing. [S11]

The RBI voted to increase the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October and changed its stance to calibrated tightening. That decision belongs in the same analytical paragraph as both food inflation and credit growth. Rate increases work through lending rates, deposit competition, housing affordability, leveraged corporate capex and discount rates with heterogeneous lags. They do not instantly reverse a food supply shock. The appropriate forecast is conditional: continued supply pressure risks more tightening; disinflation with slower domestic demand allows a pause. [S04]

## 03 / Activity: industrial strength, services resilience, uneven demand

**What the evidence means.** Factories, infrastructure-linked sectors and parts of services are holding up well, but the growth is not equally strong everywhere.

Industrial output rose 6.7% in September, slower than August's 8.0%, but manufacturing advanced 7.6% and electricity/gas supply 11.2%. The use-based detail is particularly informative: capital goods +10.4%, intermediate goods +12.2%, infrastructure/construction goods +7.2%, consumer durables +6.0%, and non-durables just +1.4%. This pattern supports a production and investment story more clearly than an across-the-board household-consumption boom. IIP is a provisional quantity index and may be revised; October IIP is scheduled for 30 November. [S05]

September core industries expanded 7.9%, up from August's revised 5.9%. Refinery products +10.3%, cement +9.8% and electricity +11.4% point to infrastructure-linked activity, yet crude oil, natural gas and fertiliser were weak. The revised August reading underlines the danger of treating a first release as immutable. [S12]

The higher-frequency surveys are more mixed. October's manufacturing PMI eased to 54.7 from 55.1, still above the expansion threshold of 50, while services PMI strengthened to 55.8 from 55.2. The composite index reached 56.2. These are diffusion readings, **not** percentage growth rates: 54.7 does not mean factory output grew by 4.7%. Services activity was supported by domestic demand, while reports of easing services input-cost pressures coexist with the official CPI acceleration. The two sources measure different populations, prices and reference periods. [S13]

MoSPI's trial Index of Services Production for August supplies additional breadth, though not an October activity measure. Retail trade was up 19.9% YoY, banking 14.9%, IT/computer-related services 13.8%, real estate 17.6% and road transport 9.4%, while air transport fell 9.6%. The category divergence matters for earnings models. A blanket “services are strong” label conceals winners and laggards. [S14]

GST adds another check. October gross receipts were ₹2.3129 lakh crore, 22.9% higher than a year earlier, including ₹1.5904 lakh crore of domestic GST (+15.8%) and ₹0.7225 lakh crore on imports (+42.2%). Net GST after refunds was ₹2.0336 lakh crore. The high import-GST contribution warns us not to treat gross GST growth as an identical growth rate for domestic real consumption. Tax rates, compliance, mix and timing also matter. [S06]

## 04 / Jobs, the household and the breadth question

**What the evidence means.** Employment indicators are improving, but inflation still matters because what households earn and what they can actually buy are not the same thing.

The latest quarterly PLFS (July–September) reported an all-India unemployment rate of 5.1%, improving from 5.4% in April–June; labour force participation was 55.5% from 54.6%, and worker-population ratio 52.7% from 51.7%. Rural unemployment fell to 4.3%, while urban unemployment was 6.8%, broadly unchanged. Female worker-population participation rose to 32.8% from 31.3% quarter-on-quarter. These are Current Weekly Status survey estimates and must not be directly substituted for older usual-status measurements or October month-end outcomes. [S15]

Higher participation alongside lower unemployment is supportive. It does not measure earnings quality, hours worked or purchasing power. Rural self-employment was 62.7% of workers, so wage growth, farm terms of trade and food purchasing power remain crucial. CPI food inflation close to 9% can make a nominal spending expansion look healthier than the typical household's experience. Consequently, consumer discretionary forecasts should cross-check unit growth, ticket size, rural distribution and credit card/loan delinquencies, not merely value growth.

## 05 / Fiscal impulses and investment

**What the evidence means.** Government spending is still supporting growth, but investors should focus on whether that spending turns into real private-sector earnings and cash flow.

The Union fiscal deficit reached approximately ₹7.14 lakh crore over April–September, compared with ₹5.73 lakh crore a year earlier. This was about 42.1% of the ₹16.96 lakh-crore full-year target, itself corresponding to a planned 4.3% of GDP. Total spending was around ₹25.5 lakh crore; government capital expenditure about ₹6.5 lakh crore, against ₹5.8 lakh crore previously. The numerator and denominator should be tracked separately: a low deficit share midway through a year does not guarantee a painless final-quarter borrowing path. [S16]

Manufacturing investment is a plausible growth channel, but the valuation test is not how many projects are announced. It is whether operating capacity is used, incremental sales materialise, and returns on invested capital exceed the cost of funding. Core infrastructure output, capital-goods production and public capex point toward the same investment mechanism, albeit over different months and frequency. Housing and commercial real-estate beneficiaries face another constraint: higher floating borrowing rates and the real-income squeeze. August's trial services index showing 17.6% growth in real-estate activity is encouraging, but is not a house-price inflation or housing-units-sold statistic. [S14]

## 06 / Credit, liquidity and what a rate hike transmits

**What the evidence means.** Credit growth is strong today, but higher interest rates can slowly squeeze margins, funding costs and future loan quality.

The RBI's September sectoral credit data showed non-food credit growth of 19.1% YoY, versus 11.2% a year earlier; industry and agriculture credit grew around 19.5%. Banks were entering the October policy tightening from a position of rapid asset growth. That helps activity but raises the importance of deposit costs, loan repricing, unsecured credit and eventual asset quality. Strong lending now can coexist with a later compression in bank net interest margins. [S07]

The RBI's October bulletin reported system credit growth above 18% in mid-September, credit-deposit competition and bank system capital ratios well above regulatory minimums. Yet system liquidity and financial stability are not synonyms: even with a strong capital buffer, a funding-cost shock can affect marginal borrowers. In company-level models, separate loan-book expansion from risk-adjusted earnings after provisioning and higher funding costs. [S17]

## 07 / Forex reserves, FCNR deposits and the cost of dollars

**What the evidence means.** India still has a large foreign-exchange buffer, but dollar funding is not free, and a weaker rupee raises costs for importers and foreign-currency borrowers.

India's foreign-exchange reserves stood at $717.06bn as of 30 October, down $2.69bn from the prior week and roughly $68.65bn from the early-September reading of $785.71bn. The level remains large, but a change in dollar-valued reserves cannot be treated mechanically as RBI spot-market intervention: FX valuation, gold prices, swaps and other transactions affect the stock. Reserve adequacy should be examined using goods-plus-services import cover, short-term external debt and prospective capital-flow pressure. [S08]

A striking special factor is the RBI's USD–INR forex-swap support for non-resident funding. Under the facility, FCNR(B) deposits mobilised through 31 August were reported at about $133.0bn, alongside approximately $5.3bn each of overseas foreign-currency borrowings and external commercial borrowings by 18 September. These are gross mobilisations under a defined swap window, **not** automatic additions to freely available permanent reserves or net balance-of-payments flows. They can change the timing, maturity and cost of India's dollar funding. Stress tests should examine the eventual unwind or rollover. [S18]

At an indicative mid-market USD/INR of around ₹94.10 on 13 November, imports priced in dollars impose a substantial INR cash requirement. Exporters with dollar receipts may benefit from translation, while unhedged importers, fuel-intensive manufacturers and foreign-currency borrowers can lose. For FCNR-linked funding, a bank or corporate treasury should model the all-in swapped rupee cost: USD deposit coupon plus forward/swap costs, collateral, liquidity and rollover premium, rather than comparing headline USD interest with the INR repo rate. [S24]

## 08 / Corporate earnings: follow unit economics, not the macro headline

**What the evidence means.** A good macro headline does not automatically mean every company will do well. The winners are the firms that convert revenue into margins and cash flow.

Corporate disclosures begin to show why the same economy can produce different earnings trajectories. Hindustan Unilever reported September-quarter turnover of ₹18,071 crore, underlying sales growth of 15%, underlying volume growth of 7%, and an EBITDA margin of 22.6%. Here the company actually discloses a volume measure; that makes the revenue-mix question empirically answerable in a way the aggregate trade headline is not. Reported profit needs separate examination of exceptional items. [S19]

TCS reported Q2 FY27 revenue of $7.642bn and 24.0% operating margin, with international constant-currency revenue growth of 1.2% quarter-on-quarter, total contract value of $9.6bn and annualised AI revenue of $3.1bn. Those indicators illuminate exportable digital-service demand, but company bookings are not the same thing as RBI monthly services exports. New contracts, utilisation, employee costs and client budget cycles determine whether deal value becomes recognised revenue and cash flow. [S20]

Infosys reported ₹49,244 crore in quarterly revenue, up 10.7% in rupees, but USD revenue was up only about 1.3% YoY and its operating margin was 21.0%. The difference illustrates how currency translation can inflate nominal INR growth relative to underlying foreign-currency activity. Management's FY27 constant-currency guidance of 1–2% warns against equating a rising national services surplus with a uniform IT-demand boom. [S21]

In banking, HDFC Bank's September-quarter net interest income was reported around ₹34,620 crore, up 9.7%, while profit rose around 4.7% and net interest margin was approximately 3.16% of total assets. Reported gross advances rose about 16.3%. Compare growth in the loan stock with the yield on assets and repricing of deposits: a faster balance sheet does not guarantee faster shareholder cash generation. All figures must remain company-specific, not extrapolated to the entire sector. [S22]

## 09 / Global transmission to India

**What the evidence means.** What happens in the US, oil markets and global funding conditions still matters a lot for India through the rupee, inflation and valuation multiples.

On 28 October, the US Federal Reserve held its policy range at 3.75–4.00%, saying US activity was strong while inflation remained elevated. Indian monetary policy therefore cannot assume immediate external easing. Higher global real yields can lift the rupee risk premium, tighten dollar-funding conditions, and lower the valuation multiple for long-duration domestic equities. The World Bank's October India Development Update projected FY27 growth of 7.1%, while highlighting oil, weather and global financial-market downside risks; this is an institutional forecast, not a subsequently observed GDP print. [S23, S25]

Elevated crude prices and the disruption of West Asian shipping routes transmit to India via four channels: import invoices, freight and insurance, domestic inflation, and profits in industries unable to pass costs through. Even if exports are at a record, the marginal dollar required for oil can raise currency and credit-market stress. A robust India thesis therefore distinguishes internationally competitive, cash-generative exporters from leveraged domestic businesses whose value depends on low rates and stable materials costs.

## 10 / A disciplined valuation bridge, not a forecast masquerading as fact

**What the evidence means.** Higher sales alone do not create value. What matters is how much cash a business keeps after costs, tax and reinvestment.

A careful valuation framework starts with the economics of the business: how fast do units sold grow; how much is price/mix; which costs move with sales; what additional capital must be invested; and how does the weighted cost of capital change? A company can produce 15% nominal revenue growth and still destroy value if reinvestment is large and incremental return on capital drops below financing cost.

The following example is **purely hypothetical**, in ₹ crore, based on current annual sales of ₹10,000 crore. It represents no listed company and is not a price target:

| One-year scenario | Constructive investment | Margin squeeze | Risk-off demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue growth (assumed) | 12% | 9% | 5% |
| Forward revenue, ₹ crore | 11,200 | 10,900 | 10,500 |
| Operating margin (assumed) | 18% | 16% | 13% |
| EBIT, ₹ crore | 2,016 | 1,744 | 1,365 |
| NOPAT after 25% tax, ₹ crore | 1,512 | 1,308 | 1,024 |
| Reinvestment / NOPAT (assumed) | 45% | 55% | 65% |
| Illustrative FCFF, ₹ crore | 832 | 589 | 358 |
| Cost of capital (assumed) | 10% | 11% | 12% |

Even a relatively small shortfall in operating margin, combined with more reinvestment and a higher discount rate, can drastically reduce the free cash flow available to the enterprise. These scenarios deliberately have no probabilities or DCF price targets. A production-grade valuation must forecast five to ten years of sales by business line, capital intensity, working-capital needs, taxes, terminal competitive returns and financing costs, with financial-statement reconciliation.

**Constructive case.** Export growth persists in real quantities; energy costs normalise; incremental investment generates returns above the cost of capital; a maturing services surplus partially offsets goods imports. Watch export shipment volumes, IIP capital goods, non-durable demand, rural real income and company free cash flow.

**Margin-pressure case.** Nominal exports and GST remain strong, but crude and food prices stay elevated; CPI is sticky; the RBI holds or tightens further; company sales grow faster than operating cash flow. Watch services margin commentary, industry input inflation, interest coverage and the funding gap between bank deposits and loans.

**Risk-off case.** External risk premiums and energy costs rise together while growth and employment weaken; the currency and capital flows absorb pressure; investment projects are deferred. Watch reserve adequacy, credit quality, government borrowing costs, foreign funding rollovers and liquidity, not merely equity-index levels.

## 11 / Release discipline and what can overturn the thesis

The cut-off is **13 November 2026, 21:00 IST**. October CPI (published 12 November), October GST (published 1 November) and October international trade (published 13 November) are available. September IIP, September WPI, September RBI services trade and the July–September PLFS quarter have later releases but refer to earlier observation periods. Q1 FY27 remains the latest published quarterly GDP; Q2 FY27 GDP, October WPI and October IIP are not available by this cut-off. [S03, S05, S09, S11]

Further updates should reconcile the complete October Commerce Ministry commodity/country table with the same statistical vintage of 2025 imports, check for revisions to preliminary services estimates, and distinguish estimates from RBI actuals. The following are **not established findings**: export growth is mainly quantity rather than prices; AI-token usage caused the rise in services imports; FTA signing caused the entire export rise to any named country; rising GST equals real domestic consumption growth. Each requires additional measurement or a credible method for identifying cause and effect.

A robust release sequence must also account for revisions: the October WPI is expected on 16 November, the October infrastructure index on 20 November, and October IIP and July–September GDP estimates toward month-end. An article published before those releases should display its cut-off and be updated with a dated change log, not silently rewritten.
